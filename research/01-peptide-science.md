**Date:** 2026-10-10

**Subject:** GHK-Cu, BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295 (no DAC) and Ipamorelin, suggested by a local GP in Columbus, Ohio for overall health.

**Purpose:** Background research behind a one-page purchasing decision. It is not medical advice. All doses in this document are commonly cited reference ranges, not recommendations.

Items the analysts could not confirm are marked "unverified".

## Evidence grade key

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | FDA-approved, or multiple RCTs for some indication |
| B | Human RCTs exist but are negative, small, or not for general-health use |
| C | Small or limited human data only |
| D | Preclinical (animal or cell) only |

Where the research gave different grades for different uses of the same peptide, both are shown.

## How the five fit together

- **Two functional units and one stand-alone.** CJC-1295 (no DAC) and ipamorelin form one growth-hormone-axis pair. BPC-157 and TB-500 form one tissue-repair pair. GHK-Cu stands alone. None of the five has been studied together in humans.
- **Most redundant pairing.** BPC-157 and TB-500 overlap most in purpose. The only direct comparison, a 2026 rat Achilles study, found no added benefit from using both.
- **Cancer caution adds up.** BPC-157, TB-500 and GHK-Cu are pro-angiogenic or pro-repair, and CJC-1295 and ipamorelin raise GH and IGF-1. Stacking them compounds the theoretical tumour-growth concern. The sources advise avoiding the stack with any cancer history, and the GP should screen for it first.
- **Blends versus separate vials.** The GHK-Cu research notes that a GLOW blend (GHK-Cu 50 mg + BPC-157 10 mg + TB-500 10 mg per vial) is cheaper and simpler but fixes the ratio. The adjacent-peptide research favours separate vials: no combination studies exist, separate vials allow independent dosing and stopping, and each gets its own certificate of analysis per batch.
- **Quality risk multiplies.** Each peptide in a stack is a separate grey-market quality risk. Batch-level third-party certificates (HPLC purity, MS identity, net content, endotoxin) should be checked for every vial type.

## 1. GHK-Cu

**Evidence grade:** C for topical skin use; D for injectable or systemic use. **Summary verdict:** best-studied of the five for skin, but almost all human evidence is topical.

### What it is

GHK-Cu is the copper(II) complex of glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine, a naturally occurring tripeptide (Gly-His-Lys) with high affinity for copper(II).

Other names and forms:

- Copper peptide GHK-Cu.
- Copper tripeptide-1 (the INCI cosmetic name).
- Prezatide copper (INN, as acetate).
- Lamin / Iamin Gel (a 1990s wound-gel brand, sold under device clearance).
- A component of "GLOW" blends (GHK-Cu 50 mg + BPC-157 10 mg + TB-500 10 mg).

Background:

- Loren Pickart isolated it in 1973 from human albumin/plasma as a factor that made old liver tissue synthesise proteins like young tissue.
- Pickart reports plasma levels of about 200 ng/mL at age 20, falling to about 80 ng/mL by age 60.
- A review notes that no study has yet linked low GHK levels to a specific aging process or disease. It is cited as Pickart 2015, PMC4508379. (The research notes call it a 2021 review, which does not match the 2015 reference. This was not resolved.)
- It is mainly a cosmetic ingredient (copper tripeptide-1) used in serums and creams.
- It is also sold as a lyophilised "research peptide" for subcutaneous injection, usually in 50 mg or 100 mg vials. The reconstituted solution is often blue.
- It is not an FDA-approved drug for any indication.

### How it is thought to work

The proposed mechanisms come mainly from in-vitro, animal and gene-expression work.

1. **Copper carrier.** It delivers bioavailable Cu2+ to cells and to copper-dependent enzymes such as lysyl oxidase (collagen and elastin cross-linking) and SOD.
2. **Matrix building.** It stimulates fibroblast synthesis of collagen, elastin and glycosaminoglycans, and modulates the MMP/TIMP balance (extracellular-matrix remodelling).
3. **Vessels and nerves.** It promotes angiogenesis and nerve outgrowth.
4. **Inflammation.** It has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects.
5. **Gene expression.** It broadly modulates gene expression.
   - In Connectivity Map analyses, GHK reversed an emphysema/COPD gene signature in lung tissue. It also restored TGF-beta-pathway-linked collagen remodelling in COPD fibroblasts (Campbell 2012, Genome Med; in vitro only).
   - Secondary sources say GHK suppressed about 70% of 54 genes overexpressed in metastatic colon cancer (Hong 2010, Clin Exp Metastasis). The original paper was not retrieved.

These are mechanistic or transcriptomic signals, not demonstrated clinical effects.

Claimed benefits, as marketed:

- Skin anti-aging: more collagen and elastin, fewer fine lines and wrinkles, better firmness and elasticity (strongest evidence, topical).
- Wound healing and tissue repair (skin; animal data also for bone, liver and stomach lining).
- Hair growth or thickening (mostly preclinical; the key 2007 study may have tested AHK-Cu, not GHK-Cu).
- Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects.
- Systemic "anti-aging" or gene-expression "resetting" (speculative, based on Connectivity Map data).
- Lung protection in COPD (in vitro gene-expression only).
- Anti-cancer or anti-metastatic gene effects (transcriptomic screen only; not shown clinically).
- Nerve outgrowth and cognitive or neuroprotective claims (preclinical or gene-expression only).

### What the evidence shows

**Human RCTs (topical only, small, often industry-linked):**

- **Leyden/Finkey (2002, conference abstract seen only second-hand).** A 12-week placebo-controlled study of topical GHK-Cu on the faces of 71 women with photodamage. The reported improvements, including a vendor-cited 67% cut in wrinkle volume, are unverified.
- **About 2017 (randomized, double-blind).** 40 women aged 40-65 over 8 weeks. A nano-carrier GHK-Cu serum reduced wrinkle volume by 31.6% versus Matrixyl 3000 (p=0.004). Funding may have been commercial (unverified).
- **Miller 2006 (Arch Facial Plast Surg 8:252-9).** An independent RCT with 13 completers after CO2 laser resurfacing. There was no significant objective improvement in redness, wrinkles or skin quality. Only patient satisfaction was higher.
- **Mulder 1994 (Wound Repair Regen 2:259-69).** A multicentre, randomized, evaluator-blinded, placebo-controlled trial of topical GHK-Cu gel in diabetic neuropathic ulcers. It reportedly showed better closure, but secondary sources quote conflicting numbers, so treat the magnitude as unverified.

**Injectable or systemic human data: none found.** Several sources say there is no completed controlled human trial, safety study or pharmacokinetic study of injected GHK-Cu. The 1-2 mg/day injection dose is a community convention, not a validated clinical dose.

**Animal data.** Systemic healing gains in rats, mice and pigs (collagen production, wound closure).

**In-vitro and transcriptomic data.** Fibroblast collagen and ECM effects; COPD-fibroblast remodelling (Campbell 2012); reversal of a colon-cancer metastasis gene signature (Hong 2010, seen only second-hand).

**Hair.** Preclinical only. No controlled human trial of GHK-Cu alone was found. The much-cited Pyo 2007 paper is reported to have tested AHK-Cu.

**Gaps.**

- No human efficacy or safety data for the injectable route the GP proposes.
- No long-term safety data.
- No data on combining it with the other four peptides.
- Most positive human results are topical, small and partly industry-funded.
- An Examine.com page for GHK-Cu could not be retrieved (unverified).

**Grade overall:** C for topical skin use, D for injectable or systemic use.

**Key studies and sources of evidence**

GHK stimulates blood-vessel and nerve outgrowth and collagen/elastin/GAG synthesis, with repair effects in skin, lung connective tissue, bone, liver and stomach lining. ### Reference protocols

*Commonly cited reference ranges to discuss with the prescribing GP, not a recommendation. These are community and vendor conventions, not validated clinical doses. No human trial supports any injectable dose.*

- **Subcutaneous injection:** 1-2 mg once daily is the most commonly cited dose.
- **Perfect B (vendor/provider guide):** 1 mg/day (4 units at 25 mg/mL) on days 1-15, then 2 mg/day (8 units) on days 16-30; 30 days on, 30 days off.
- **Community wiki:** 1-2 mg daily, 4-8 weeks on, 2-4 weeks off. Its calculator also mentions every-other-day dosing.
- **Other sources:** 5 days on and 2 off for 8-12 weeks.
- **Timing:** no evidence-based timing. Evenings are often used in community practice (unverified).
- **Topical alternative, the only route with human RCT data:** commercial creams or serums applied once or twice daily for 8-12 weeks. Concentrations vary by product, and the trials did not specify a standard.
- **Monitoring suggested by a community wiki:** baseline serum copper and ceruloplasmin, then serum copper at about 4 weeks for injectable use. Stop if copper is elevated.
- **Reconstitution example (arithmetic):** a 50 mg vial plus 2 mL bacteriostatic water gives 25 mg/mL, so 1 mg is 4 units on a U-100 insulin syringe. With 1 mL of water, 1 mg is 2 units.

### Side effects and contraindications

**Topical (documented):** mild, temporary redness or irritation; rare allergic contact dermatitis.

**Injectable:** no human safety data exist.

- Stinging or pain at the injection site is widely reported anecdotally (unverified, no study).
- FDA's stated concern, as reported in secondary summaries, is immunogenicity from aggregation and peptide-related impurities in compounded injectable product, rather than copper toxicity. This is why injectable GHK-Cu was placed in 503A Category 2 in 2023.
- There is a theoretical risk of copper overload with heavy or long-term use (GI upset; liver or kidney effects in overload).
- Research-grade injectable product: sterility and endotoxin are not guaranteed. "Research use only" vials are not made under drug GMP.

**Contraindications and cautions:**

- Wilson's disease or other copper-metabolism disorders: avoid.
- Pregnancy and breastfeeding: insufficient data, avoid.
- Active cancer or cancer history: theoretical caution, because GHK-Cu promotes angiogenesis and tissue growth. Pro-angiogenic healing peptides are generally flagged for people with tumours. Some gene data suggest anti-metastatic effects, but no clinical data exist either way.
- Known copper allergy or sensitivity.
- Tested athletes: GHK-Cu is not named on the WADA Prohibited List, but it may be caught by the S0 "non-approved substances" catch-all (unverified against WADA's text).

**Drug interactions:** none documented. Effects are theoretically additive with copper supplements. Zinc/copper balance has been raised as a question (unverified).

### Interactions with the other four

- **BPC-157 and TB-500.** Strong overlap in purpose (tissue repair, wound healing, angiogenesis).
  - Vendors sell the three together as the "GLOW" blend, typically 50 mg GHK-Cu + 10 mg BPC-157 + 10 mg TB-500 per 70 mg vial, for skin and recovery.
  - Any synergy is marketing or theory only. The blend is untested in humans, and one vendor warns that the combination's behaviour should not be assumed to equal the parts.
  - Because all three are pro-angiogenic or pro-repair, the overlap adds to the theoretical concern for anyone with a cancer history.
  - Buying GLOW instead of three separate vials is cheaper and simpler, but it fixes the ratio, so GHK-Cu (the dominant mass) cannot be dosed independently.
- **CJC-1295 (no DAC) and ipamorelin.** These act on the growth-hormone axis (a GHRH analogue plus a ghrelin-receptor agonist). There is no mechanistic overlap, no known pharmacological interaction or conflict with GHK-Cu, and no data on combined use. GH/IGF-1 elevation plus pro-angiogenic peptides compounds the theoretical oncologic caution.
- **Practical point.** GHK-Cu is often injected separately (or used topically) because of copper colour and stinging (anecdotal). The topical route avoids most regulatory and sterility issues and has the only human RCT data, so it is a reasonable substitute for the injectable component.

### Handling and storage

- **Vial sizes commonly sold:** 50 mg lyophilised (most common) and 100 mg. Some vendors list 10 mg or 200 mg. It is also sold inside 70 mg GLOW blends (50 mg of which is GHK-Cu).
- **Reconstitution:** inject bacteriostatic water (0.9% benzyl alcohol) slowly down the vial wall and swirl gently. Do not shake. Common volumes are 2 mL for a 50 mg vial (25 mg/mL) or 1 mL (50 mg/mL). Use 2-4 mL for 100 mg.
- **Lyophilised storage:** -20 °C to 4 °C, dry, away from light.
- **Reconstituted storage:** refrigerate at 2-8 °C and do not freeze. Most vendor sources give 21-30 days; one gives 2 weeks and one gives 30-45 days. With sterile (non-bacteriostatic) water, about 5-7 days. Protect from light (amber vial or foil).
- **Colour:** fresh solution is described as light to deep blue, which is normal. A brown tint or cloudiness suggests degradation (vendor claims, unverified).
- **Practical implication:** at 1 mg/day, a 50 mg vial (50 days of supply) outlasts the roughly 28-day reconstituted window. A GP may prefer reconstituting with more water and accepting some discard, or using smaller vials if available.

### Verdict for general health

GHK-Cu is the best-studied of the five for skin, but almost all of the human evidence is topical. Small topical RCTs show modest wrinkle and collagen improvements, and the one independent trial was largely negative. No human trial covers the injectable route the GP proposes, so injected use for "overall health" rests on animal, in-vitro and gene-expression data (grade D).

Its risk profile appears low as a topical cosmetic. For injection, the open questions are sterility and immunogenicity of research-grade vials, with copper load as a theoretical concern.

Regulatory position as of October 2026: the GP cannot currently get a lawfully compounded injectable from a 503A pharmacy, because FDA removed GHK-Cu from both categories in April 2026 and plans a PCAC review before the end of February 2027. (This is the research team's inference from the sources and should be checked against the FDA page.) "Research use only" purchase is the only injectable route, and FDA is actively sending warning letters to such sellers, especially those bundling bacteriostatic water.

Reasonable positioning, from the research:

- **Lowest-risk option with the most evidence:** a topical GHK-Cu product.
- **If injecting on the GP's advice:** about 1-2 mg/day subcutaneously for 4-12 weeks, 2 x 50 mg vials, a vendor with lot-specific third-party HPLC/MS certificates of analysis, and baseline and 4-week serum copper.
- **Avoid** with Wilson's disease, pregnancy, or active or prior cancer.
- **Overlap:** it largely overlaps with BPC-157 and TB-500 rather than adding something distinct, so the GLOW blend is a cost-efficient way to buy all three if the GP endorses the fixed 50:10:10 ratio.

## 2. BPC-157

**Evidence grade:** D for the stated purpose (general health in a healthy adult). The narrow indications of knee pain and interstitial cystitis would reach at most C- on the basis of tiny uncontrolled pilots.

### What it is

BPC-157 (Body Protection Compound-157, "stable gastric pentadecapeptide") is a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide.

Other names:

- BPC 157; Body Protection Compound 157; stable gastric pentadecapeptide BPC 157; Bepecin.
- PL 14736 / PL-10 / PLD-116 (Pliva development codes).
- PCO-02 (the oral tablet code in NCT02637284).
- BPC-157 acetate; BPC-157 arginine salt (an oral "stable" form).
- The "Wolverine peptide" (media nickname).

Background:

- It is a partial sequence of a protein ("body protection compound") found in human gastric juice.
- It was developed in Croatia, mainly by the Sikiric group, with the pharmaceutical company Pliva.
- Pliva took it into early clinical trials for inflammatory bowel disease in the 2000s, but full efficacy results were never published.
- In the US it is sold mostly as a lyophilised "research use only" powder (acetate salt, sometimes oral arginine salt).
- It is not approved as a drug anywhere.

### How it is thought to work

The mechanisms come almost entirely from rodent and in-vitro work.

1. **Angiogenesis and the nitric-oxide system.** In human endothelial cells and a rat hind-limb ischemia model, BPC-157 raised VEGFR2 mRNA and protein and activated VEGFR2-Akt-eNOS signalling. Several authors also describe it as modulating NO in both directions.
2. **Tendon healing.** In rat Achilles models and cultured tendon fibroblasts it activated FAK-paxillin. It increased fibroblast outgrowth, migration and survival under oxidative stress, and did not directly increase proliferation.
3. **Growth hormone receptor.** Chang et al. 2014 (Molecules 19:19066) found that BPC-157 raised GH-receptor mRNA and protein in rat tendon fibroblasts in a dose- and time-dependent way. When GH was added, these cells showed more JAK2 activation and more proliferation. This is in vitro only.
4. **Cytoprotection and gut protection.** These effects are the basis of the original anti-ulcer and IBD programme.
5. **Other pathways.** A 2025 AAOS abstract also lists KRAS and MAPK (proliferation) among the pathways BPC-157 affects.

**Pharmacokinetics** (He et al. 2022, Frontiers in Pharmacology, rats and dogs):

- Elimination half-life under 30 min (15.2 min IV in rats).
- Linear kinetics.
- Intramuscular bioavailability 14-19% in rats and 45-51% in dogs.
- Broken down into small fragments and amino acids, and excreted in urine and bile.
- No human PK data have been published.

Claimed benefits, as marketed:

- Faster healing of tendon, ligament, muscle and bone injuries (strong animal data, no controlled human data).
- Joint pain relief, for example knee (one small uncontrolled human chart review).
- Gut protection and healing: ulcers, IBD/ulcerative colitis, leaky gut (rodent data and an unpublished Pliva phase II).
- Interstitial cystitis / bladder pain (one 12-patient uncontrolled pilot).
- Systemic anti-inflammatory and "cytoprotective" effects.
- Angiogenesis and wound healing.
- Protection of organs (liver, brain, blood vessels) from various toxins (rodent data only).
- General "recovery" and "overall health" (no human evidence).

### What the evidence shows

**Human RCTs: none published.**

- Pliva ran a phase I rectal-enema study in healthy men (abstract in Gut 2003, reported as safe and well tolerated).
- Pliva also ran a phase II in mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis. The phase II results exist only as conference abstracts.
- NCT02637284 (a 2015 phase I of the oral tablet PCO-02) was never published. Secondary sources say the registry lists it as cancelled.

**Small human studies (all uncontrolled, about 30 people in total):**

- **Lee & Padgett 2021 (Altern Ther Health Med).** A retrospective chart review of 16 knee-pain patients, 12 of whom received BPC-157 alone, given as intra-articular injections. About 87.5% reported improvement. This was based on phone surveys with no control group and no validated outcome measures.
- **Lee, Walker & Ayadi 2024.** An interstitial cystitis pilot in 12 women, each given one 10 mg injection into the bladder wall during cystoscopy. 10 of 12 reported complete symptom resolution. No adverse events were reported and there was no control arm.
- **2025 intravenous safety pilot.** 2 healthy adults given up to 20 mg IV. No adverse events and no changes in vital signs, ECG or laboratory tests. Far too small to establish safety.

**Systematic and scoping reviews:**

- Vasireddi et al. 2025 (HSS Journal) included 36 studies, of which 35 were preclinical and 1 clinical. Preclinical results consistently suggest healing benefits for fractures, tendon, ligament and muscle injuries, but human efficacy is not established.
- McGuire et al. 2025 (Curr Rev Musculoskelet Med 18:611-619) scoped mechanisms, therapeutic potential and safety concerns.
- A 2026 primer in the American Journal of Sports Medicine (Mayfield et al., vol 54 no 1) covers BPC-157, TB-4/TB-500, CJC-1295 with ipamorelin, tesamorelin and GHK-Cu. It describes the human benefit as unsubstantiated. The analysts could see this only through a secondary summary.

**Animal and in-vitro data.** A large rodent literature, mostly from a single Croatian group, which limits independent replication. It shows tendon, muscle, bone, gut, vascular and CNS protection at µg/kg doses that do not translate directly to human doses.

**Gaps.**

- No dose-finding studies.
- No long-term safety data.
- No human PK.
- No independent replication.
- No evidence for "overall health" use in healthy adults.
- No human cancer-risk data. The theoretical concern comes from its pro-angiogenic and proliferative signalling.

**Grade:** D for the user's stated purpose. The narrow indications of knee pain and interstitial cystitis would reach at most C- on the basis of tiny uncontrolled pilots. (The Examine.com BPC-157 page could not be retrieved, so its content is unverified.)

### Reference protocols

*Commonly cited reference ranges to discuss with the prescribing GP, not a recommendation. There is no validated human dose. These are community and clinic practice patterns, not trial-derived doses.*

- **Route:** subcutaneous injection is most common, often near the injured area or into abdominal fat. Some clinics use intramuscular injection. Oral forms (arginine salt or acetate capsules) are marketed for gut-focused use. Intra-articular and intravesical (bladder) routes appear only in the small clinical pilots and are clinician procedures.
- **Dose:** 200-500 mcg once or twice daily is the range most often cited.
  - Rite Aid's peptide page describes 200-500 mcg once or twice daily for 4-6 weeks as a reported pattern, not an established dose.
  - The Peptide Association clinician guide cites 250-500 mcg/day for systemic use, sometimes split twice daily.
  - One clinic blog suggests starting at 250 mcg/day for 1-2 weeks.
- **Timing:** no evidence-based timing. Twice-daily splits are a common community practice because of the very short animal half-life.
- **Cycle length:** usually 4-6 weeks, sometimes up to 8-12 weeks, then stop and reassess. Clinician guides warn that long-term continuous use has not been studied.
- **Reconstitution example (arithmetic, not a medical instruction):** a 10 mg vial plus 2.0 mL bacteriostatic water gives 5 mg/mL. On a U-100 insulin syringe, 250 mcg is 0.05 mL (5 units) and 500 mcg is 0.10 mL (10 units). A 5 mg vial plus 2.0 mL gives 2.5 mg/mL, so 250 mcg is 0.10 mL (10 units).

### Side effects and contraindications

**Reported or anecdotal side effects:** injection-site redness or irritation, mild headache, nausea, dizziness and flushing.

- The published pilots of about 30 people reported no adverse events, but they were not designed to detect them.
- Long-term effects are unknown.
- USADA says it "has not been extensively studied in humans, so no one knows if there is a safe dose".

**FDA concerns** (from the Category 2 rationale dated 29 Sep 2023): possible immunogenicity for some routes, peptide-related impurities, difficulty characterising the active ingredient, and little or no human safety data for the proposed routes. In its May 2026 PCAC briefing, FDA staff again proposed NOT adding BPC-157 to the 503A list on safety and efficacy grounds.

**Theoretical cancer concern.** BPC-157 drives pro-angiogenic (VEGFR2/VEGF), proliferative (FAK-paxillin, KRAS, MAPK) and GH-receptor signalling. It is commonly flagged as a relative or absolute contraindication with active cancer, a history of cancer, or known precancerous lesions. There are no human data either way.

**Other cautions:**

- Pregnancy and breastfeeding: no data, avoid.
- Under 18.
- Competitive athletes, including amateurs: WADA S0 ban. A USADA four-year ban was given to a 46-year-old age-group triathlete in Sep 2025.
- Military personnel: on the DoD prohibited-ingredient list via OPSS.

**Product-quality risks,** arguably the largest real-world risk:

- Grey-market vials can be under- or over-dosed, mislabelled, empty, or contaminated with endotoxin.
- Finnrick audit data show about 41.6% failing on dose, purity or identity, and 15% of endotoxin-tested vials positive.
- Fabricated certificates of analysis exist, so verify them through the third-party lab.
- Non-sterile handling risks infection.

**Drug interactions:** no formal studies. Rodent work suggests BPC-157 interacts with the NO system and can counteract some drug toxicities, but the clinical relevance is unknown. Tell the GP about anticoagulants, antihypertensives and any oncology history.

### Interactions with the other four

- **TB-500 (thymosin beta-4 fragment).** The most common pairing (the "Wolverine" stack).
  - The mechanisms are proposed to complement each other: TB-500 regulates actin and cell migration, while BPC-157 works through angiogenesis, NO, FAK-paxillin and the GH receptor.
  - This is synergistic in theory for soft-tissue repair, but no human study has tested the combination. Only the 2021 knee chart review included some BPC-157 + TB4 patients, without separate analysis.
  - Both promote angiogenesis, so stacking them adds to the theoretical tumour-promotion concern.
  - Both were removed from FDA Category 2 in April 2026, and both received favourable (non-binding) PCAC votes in July 2026. Both are banned by WADA.
- **GHK-Cu.** Complementary rather than redundant. GHK-Cu is mainly marketed for collagen, skin and hair remodelling and copper-dependent enzymes, while BPC-157 is aimed at tendons, the gut and blood vessels. Commercial pre-mixed blends combine them: "GLOW" is about 50 mg GHK-Cu + 10 mg BPC-157 + 10 mg TB-500 per vial, and "KLOW" adds 10 mg KPV. These blends fix the ratios, so the doses cannot be adjusted independently. The vial compositions come from vendor listings and are unverified.
- **CJC-1295 (no DAC) and ipamorelin.** These are GH secretagogues that raise pulsatile GH and IGF-1.
  - BPC-157 upregulates the GH receptor in tendon fibroblasts and increases GH-driven proliferation in vitro (Chang 2014), so there is a plausible mechanistic synergy for connective-tissue repair. There is no human data on the combination.
  - The flip side is that layering GH/IGF-1 elevation on top of BPC-157's pro-angiogenic and GH-receptor-sensitising effects compounds the theoretical cancer concern. The combination should be avoided with any cancer history.
- **Redundancy.** BPC-157 and TB-500 overlap the most in purpose (tissue repair). For "general health" with no injury, the rationale for BPC-157 is the weakest of the list's healing peptides. A GP might reasonably prioritise one repair peptide and add the second only for a specific injury.
- **Practical point.** Each peptide in a stack is a separate grey-market quality risk, so batch-level third-party certificates (HPLC purity, MS identity, net content, endotoxin) should be checked for every vial type.

### Handling and storage

- **Vial sizes commonly sold in the US:** 5 mg and 10 mg lyophilised vials are standard. Some vendors also sell 15, 20 and 40 mg, oral capsules, and pre-mixed blends (GLOW about 70 mg, KLOW about 80 mg).
- **Reconstitution:** inject bacteriostatic water (0.9% benzyl alcohol) slowly down the inside wall of the vial, then swirl gently. Do not shake. 2 mL per 5 or 10 mg is the usual community convention.
- **Lyophilised storage:** about -20 °C, dry and protected from light, for long-term storage (a supplier certificate of analysis and Prospec). Prospec says the powder is stable at room temperature for about 3 weeks, so normal shipping is tolerated. One vendor claims 24+ months at -20 °C and 12-18 months at 2-8 °C (unverified).
- **Reconstituted storage:** refrigerate at 2-8 °C and do not freeze-thaw repeatedly. In-use stability estimates conflict widely: Prospec says 2-7 days at 4 °C, other vendors say 14 days, HealthRx says 28-30 days, and some vendors say 30-45 days. No peer-reviewed stability study exists, so a conservative window of 2-4 weeks is safer.
- **Matching vial size to use:** choose a vial size so that each vial is used up within that window. At 250 mcg/day, a 5 mg vial lasts 20 days and a 10 mg vial lasts 40 days.
- **Supplies:** sterile insulin syringes and alcohol swabs.

### Verdict for general health

Weak case for a healthy adult seeking "overall health".

- BPC-157 has impressive and consistent rodent data for tendon, ligament, muscle, gut and vascular healing.
- Human evidence is limited to about 30 people in three uncontrolled pilots, plus unpublished Pliva IBD trials.
- There is no evidence for general wellness, longevity or prevention in healthy people, and no long-term safety data.
- Its pro-angiogenic and GH-receptor-sensitising mechanisms create a theoretical cancer concern, which grows when it is stacked with TB-500 and CJC-1295/ipamorelin.

It is most defensible as a time-limited (4-8 week) trial for a specific musculoskeletal or GI problem the GP is monitoring, not as an ongoing wellness agent.

Legally it remains an unapproved drug. It came off FDA Category 2 in April 2026 and won a non-binding 8-6 PCAC vote in July 2026, but it is not yet on the 503A list. If the GP's prescription route matures, a 503A-compounded product would offer better quality assurance than a grey-market "research use only" vial.

If buying "research use only", the research advises:

- Insist on batch-matched third-party certificates: HPLC purity of 98% or more, MS identity, net peptide content, and ideally endotoxin.
- Check them against the lab's own site.
- Buy from vendors with good independent (for example Finnrick) ratings.

Avoid with any cancer history, during pregnancy, or if subject to WADA or military testing. **Grade D for this purpose.**

## 3. TB-500

**Evidence grade:** D. For the product actually sold, the evidence is animal and in-vitro work plus anecdote. Full-length thymosin beta-4 alone (a different molecule) would rate about C.

### What it is

TB-500 is a synthetic peptide, sold as a "synthetic thymosin beta-4 fragment".

Other names:

- TB-500; TB500.
- Thymosin Beta-4 Fragment (LKKTETQ); Ac-LKKTETQ; N-acetyl Tβ4(17-23).
- Often (mis)labelled by vendors as "Thymosin Beta-4" or "TB4".
- Related but distinct molecules: full-length thymosin beta-4 (43 amino acids), RGN-259 (eye drops), RGN-137 (topical gel), RGN-352 (injectable) and NL005 (recombinant Tβ4).

What it actually is:

- The material found in TB-500 products was identified as the N-terminally acetylated 7-residue fragment Ac-LKKTETQ. These are residues 17-23 of human thymosin beta-4 (Tβ4) and the core of its actin-binding region (Esposito et al., Drug Test Anal 2012).
- The FDA PCAC briefing document (fda.gov/media/193349) describes TB-500 as "a seven amino acid synthetic fragment of thymosin beta-4 from amino acids 17 to 23 with an acetyl group at the N-terminal leucine". It notes that "TB-500" is a common name, not a USAN, and says FDA has seen multiple salts and derivatives sold under the name.
- Full-length Tβ4 is a different molecule: a 43-amino-acid protein made naturally in platelets, macrophages and most cells.
- Nearly all of the human clinical data belong to full-length Tβ4 (RegeneRx/ReGenTree RGN-259, RGN-137, RGN-352), not to the TB-500 fragment.
- Vendors use "TB-500" and "Thymosin Beta-4" loosely, so what is in a given vial (the 7-mer or the full 43-mer) can only be confirmed from that lot's mass-spec certificate.

### How it is thought to work

- Full-length Tβ4 is the main G-actin-sequestering peptide in cells. It binds monomeric actin, which supports cell migration, and it promotes endothelial-cell migration and angiogenesis.
- It also lowers inflammation and apoptosis after injury and reduces myofibroblast numbers in wounds, which may mean less scarring (Goldstein, Hannappel, Sosne, Kleinman, Expert Opin Biol Ther 2012;12(1):37-51).
- In mouse hearts, Tβ4 forms a complex with PINCH and integrin-linked kinase (ILK) that activates the survival kinase Akt. This improved cardiomyocyte survival and cardiac function after coronary ligation (Bock-Marquette et al., Nature 2004;432:466-472).
- Philp et al. (FASEB J 2003;17(12), "The actin binding site on thymosin beta4 promotes angiogenesis") reported that the actin-binding region alone is angiogenic. This is the reason the 17-23 fragment is sold as TB-500.
- Other activities of full-length Tβ4 come from different parts of the molecule, for example the anti-fibrotic N-terminal tetrapeptide Ac-SDKP. The 7-mer fragment would not be expected to have those activities. That is an inference, and no head-to-head human data exist.
- In horses, TB-500 is metabolised mainly by stepwise loss of C-terminal amino acids (Ho et al., J Chromatogr A 2012).

Claimed benefits, as marketed:

- Faster healing of soft tissue (tendon, ligament, muscle) and recovery from injury.
- Wound and skin healing.
- Less inflammation and scarring/fibrosis.
- Angiogenesis (new blood vessels) in injured tissue.
- Cardiac repair after ischaemia (animal data for full-length Tβ4).
- Corneal and ocular surface healing (human data for full-length Tβ4 eye drops only).
- Hair growth (animal data for full-length Tβ4).
- Joint mobility and general "recovery" (community anecdote).

### What the evidence shows

**For TB-500 itself (the Ac-LKKTETQ fragment, injected subcutaneously):** no published human efficacy trials. FDA states it "has not identified any human exposure data" for drug products containing Thymosin Beta-4 Fragment (FDA "Certain Bulk Drug Substances ... Significant Safety Risks" page). The evidence for the fragment is in-vitro and animal work:

- Philp 2003 found the actin-binding region angiogenic in vitro.
- A 2026 rat Achilles-transection study (Biçer et al., Joint Dis Relat Surg 37(3):822-837; n=32; TB-500 60 µg/kg/day IP for 4 weeks) found better histology for TB-500 and for BPC-157, a biomechanical advantage only for TB-500 alone, and no added benefit from combining them.
- A 2026 scoping review (McGuire, Hughes, Maak, Cushman, Applied Sciences 16(12):6202) screened 1,772 records and included 80 studies. It found the evidence base weighted toward in-vitro and mixed designs, and said most studies tested full-length Tβ4, not TB-500.

**For full-length Tβ4 (a different molecule, so these results cannot simply be applied to TB-500):**

1. **Phase 1 IV safety study in 40 healthy volunteers** (Ruff et al., Ann N Y Acad Sci 2010;1194:223-229; NCT00743769). Randomized, placebo-controlled, single and multiple doses. Secondary sources report it was well tolerated up to about 1,260 mg with no dose-limiting toxicity. The exact dose schedule is unverified because the primary text could not be opened.
2. **A 2021 first-in-human phase 1 of recombinant Tβ4 (NL005)** in Chinese volunteers reported no dose-limiting toxicities or serious adverse events (PMC8419156).
3. **Eye drops (RGN-259).**
   - ARISE-3, a phase 3 dry-eye trial in about 700 patients, missed its primary endpoints, with some secondary and post-hoc signals.
   - In SEER-1 (neurotrophic keratopathy), complete healing at 4 weeks was 6/10 vs 1/8 on placebo (Fisher p=0.0656, not significant). That trial stopped early with n=18. No serious adverse events.
4. **Topical gel (RGN-137).** Phase 2 trials in venous stasis ulcers and pressure ulcers (n=72) showed no statistically significant difference from placebo in complete healing, and were safe.
5. **Animal work.** Bock-Marquette 2004 (mouse MI), and wound-healing and hair-follicle studies.

**Gaps.**

- No RCT of injected TB-500 for any outcome.
- No long-term human safety data.
- No data in healthy people taking it for "general health".
- Unclear what is in grey-market products.
- No human data on the BPC-157 + TB-500 combination.

**Grade D.** For the product actually sold, the evidence is animal and in-vitro work plus anecdote. Full-length Tβ4 alone would rate about C (small human trials, mostly negative or borderline on efficacy, reassuring on short-term safety).

**Key studies and sources of evidence**

- **Esposito S, et al. Synthesis and characterization of the N-terminal acetylated 17-23 fragment of thymosin beta 4 identified in TB-500. Drug Test Anal 2012 (PMID 23084823).** Identified the active content of commercial TB-500 as Ac-LKKTETQ (Tβ4 residues 17-23), not full-length Tβ4, and synthesised it to confirm the structure. ([link](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23084823/>))
- **FDA Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee briefing document: TB-500-related bulk drug substances (2026).** FDA describes TB-500 as an acetylated 7-aa Tβ4(17-23) fragment, a common name rather than a USAN, sold as multiple salts/derivatives. The nomination's proposed use was wound healing, and the nominator gave inconsistent information about which substance was meant. FDA staff recommended against adding it to the bulks list. ([link](<https://www.fda.gov/media/193349/download>))
- **FDA. Certain Bulk Drug Substances for Use in Compounding that May Present Significant Safety Risks (Thymosin Beta-4, Fragment (LKKTETQ) entry).** Compounded products 'may pose risk for immunogenicity for certain routes of administration due to the potential for aggregation as well as peptide-related impurities'. FDA has not identified any human exposure data. ([link](<https://fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/certain-bulk-drug-substances-use-compounding-may-present-significant-safety-risks>))
- **Ruff D, Crockford D, Girardi G, Zhang Y. A randomized, placebo-controlled, single and multiple dose study of intravenous thymosin beta4 in healthy volunteers. Ann N Y Acad Sci 2010;1194:223-229 (NCT00743769).** Phase 1 in 40 healthy volunteers. Full-length Tβ4 IV was reported well tolerated with no dose-limiting toxicity. The upper dose figure (about 1,260 mg) is from secondary sources and unverified against the primary text. ([link](<https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00743769>))
- **Bock-Marquette I, et al. Thymosin β4 activates integrin-linked kinase and promotes cardiac cell migration, survival and cardiac repair. Nature 2004;432:466-472.** In mice after coronary ligation, Tβ4 increased ILK/Akt activity, improved early myocyte survival and improved cardiac function. Animal data for full-length Tβ4 only. ([link](<https://www.nature.com/articles/nature03000>))
- **Goldstein AL, Hannappel E, Sosne G, Kleinman HK. Thymosin β4: a multi-functional regenerative peptide. Expert Opin Biol Ther 2012;12(1):37-51.** Review: Tβ4 binds actin, promotes cell migration, angiogenesis and stem/progenitor-cell differentiation, and reduces inflammation, apoptosis and myofibroblasts. Clinical development covered skin, eye, heart and CNS. ([link](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22074294/>))
- **Philp D, Huff T, Gho YS, Hannappel E, Kleinman HK. The actin binding site on thymosin beta4 promotes angiogenesis. FASEB J 2003;17(12).** The actin-binding region of Tβ4 (the basis for the TB-500 fragment) promoted endothelial migration and angiogenesis in vitro and ex vivo. ([link](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14517430>))
- **Biçer et al. Effects of BPC-157 and TB-500 on Achilles tendon healing in rats. Joint Dis Relat Surg 2026;37(3):822-837.** n=32 rats, 4 weeks IP. Both peptides improved histology. A biomechanical advantage was seen only for TB-500 alone, and the combination gave no added benefit over either peptide alone. ([link](<https://jointdrs.org/abstract/1851>))
- **McGuire F, Hughes E, Maak T, Cushman DM. Thymosin Beta-4 and TB-500 in Tissue Healing, Regeneration, and Musculoskeletal Repair: A Scoping Review. Applied Sciences 2026;16(12):6202.** 80 included studies, mostly in vitro and mixed designs, mostly testing full-length Tβ4 rather than TB-500. Notes WADA prohibits Tβ4 and derivatives including TB-500. ([link](<https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/thymosin-beta-4-and-tb-500-in-tissue-healing-regeneration-and-musculoskeletal-repair-a-scoping-review/>))
- **SEER-1 phase 3, 0.1% RGN-259 (Tβ4) eye drops in neurotrophic keratopathy, Int J Mol Sci 2022/2023;24(1):554.** Complete healing at 4 weeks was 6/10 vs 1/8 on placebo (Fisher p=0.0656, primary endpoint not significant). - **RegeneRx RGN-137 (topical Tβ4) phase 2 trials in venous stasis ulcers and pressure ulcers (company releases).** Safe at all doses, but no statistically significant difference from placebo in complete wound healing. ([link](<https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/regenerx-reports-phase-ii-pressure-ulcer-trial-results>))
- **ARISE-3 phase 3, RGN-259 in dry eye (about 700 patients).** Did not meet primary outcome measures. Secondary endpoint (ocular grittiness) and post-hoc subgroup signals only. No serious AEs. ([link](<https://www.ophthalmologytimes.com/view/results-of-dry-eye-trial-released>))
- **Ho EN, et al. Detection of TB-500 (N-acetylated LKKTETQ) and metabolites in equine urine and plasma, J Chromatogr A 2012.** Confirmed TB-500 and its metabolites in horses after a single dose. Metabolism is mainly sequential C-terminal loss of amino acids. Relevant to doping detection. ([link](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23084823/>))
- **Tβ4 and cancer: colorectal (143 patients, 2017), breast (70 patients) and review 'Thymosin β4: a potential molecular target for tumor therapy' (2012).** High Tβ4 expression is associated with lymph-node and distant metastasis and poorer survival in colorectal and breast cancer (observational). Proposed mechanisms are VEGF/HIF-1α and β-catenin signalling. Causality is not established. ([link](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22856429/>))

### Reference protocols

*Commonly cited reference ranges to discuss with the prescribing GP, not a recommendation. These are community and vendor conventions, not validated by any human trial of TB-500, and there is no FDA-approved dose.*

- **Route:** subcutaneous injection (abdomen or near the injury) with a U-100 insulin syringe. Some community users use IM. The only human data (full-length Tβ4) are IV, topical and ophthalmic.
- **Loading phase (most commonly cited):** 2.0-2.5 mg twice weekly (about 4-5 mg/week) for 4-6 weeks.
- **Maintenance:** about 2-2.5 mg once weekly, or 1-2 mg weekly in some sources, for a further 4-6 weeks. Sources disagree.
- **Timing:** no evidence-based timing. Doses are usually spread evenly through the week (for example Mon/Thu). Not tied to meals.
- **Cycle length:** commonly 8-12 weeks in total, then a break of several weeks. There are no data on long-term or continuous use.
- **Reconstitution example:** a 10 mg vial with 2 mL bacteriostatic water gives 5 mg/mL, so 2.5 mg is 0.5 mL (50 units) and 2 mg is 0.4 mL (40 units) on a U-100 syringe.
- **For comparison,** the rat tendon study used 60 µg/kg/day IP (Biçer 2026). Converting animal doses to human doses is not straightforward.

### Side effects and contraindications

**Reported or plausible side effects:** injection-site redness, itching or bruising; headache, lethargy/fatigue and transient flushing. These are anecdotal and community reports, not from controlled TB-500 trials.

- In the full-length Tβ4 human trials (IV phase 1, topical, ophthalmic), no dose-limiting toxicity or serious drug-related adverse events were reported.
- FDA's specific concern for the fragment is immunogenicity from aggregation and peptide-related impurities (the Category 2 rationale).
- Grey-market products add further risks: wrong identity (7-mer vs 43-mer vs something else), under- or over-filled vials, and bacterial or endotoxin contamination when products lack lot-specific HPLC, MS and LAL testing.

**Contraindications and cautions:**

1. **Active cancer or a history of cancer.** Tβ4 promotes angiogenesis and cell migration, and high Tβ4 expression is associated with metastasis and poor prognosis in colorectal and breast cancer (observational). The risk is theoretical, but most sources advise avoiding it. The caution is the same as for GH secretagogues.
2. **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** no data, avoid.
3. **Under 18:** no data.
4. **Competitive or tested athletes:** banned by WADA at all times.
5. **Known proliferative disease,** for example proliferative diabetic retinopathy or unexplained masses: theoretical, because of the angiogenic effect.
6. **Active infection at the injection site.**

**Drug interactions:** no known pharmacokinetic interactions are documented (no human interaction studies). Tell the GP about anticoagulants or antiplatelets (bruising at injection sites) and any oncology history.

### Interactions with the other four

- **BPC-157.** The most redundant pairing. Both are taken for tissue healing (the "Wolverine stack").
  - The only direct comparison, a 2026 rat Achilles study (Biçer et al.), found no added benefit from the combination over either peptide alone. The authors suggested the two converge on shared downstream pathways.
  - Many vendors sell pre-mixed BPC-157/TB-500 blend vials, which fixes the dose ratio. One vendor's certificates for such blends showed contents above label claim, which is worth checking.
  - There is no human combination data.
  - If the GP's goal is general health rather than a specific injury, one of the two may be enough.
- **GHK-Cu.** Partly overlapping wound-healing, angiogenesis and tissue-remodelling claims. GHK-Cu is mainly a skin, collagen and copper-carrier peptide. There is no interaction data. Combination blends (for example GHK-Cu + BPC-157 + TB-500, marketed as "GLOW") exist from vendors. There is no evidence of additive benefit.
- **CJC-1295 (no DAC) and ipamorelin.** A different mechanism (a GHRH analogue plus a ghrelin-receptor agonist that raises endogenous GH/IGF-1). Not redundant with TB-500, and no known pharmacokinetic interaction.
  - Concern: adding a pro-angiogenic or pro-migratory peptide (TB-500) to a GH/IGF-1-raising stack, plus BPC-157 (also pro-angiogenic in animals), stacks theoretical growth-signalling risk.
  - This matters most for anyone with a personal or strong family history of cancer, and the GP should screen for it.
- **Practical point.** None of the five has human data in combination. Each extra peptide adds cost, injections and unknowns. Do not mix separately reconstituted peptides in one syringe unless the GP agrees, because compatibility data do not exist.

### Handling and storage

- **Vial sizes commonly sold:** lyophilised powder in 2 mg, 5 mg and 10 mg vials. 10 mg is the most common retail size and 5 mg is common. 2 mg is mostly from chemical suppliers such as TargetMol. BPC-157/TB-500 blend vials (10 mg, 20 mg) also exist.
- **Reconstitution:** add bacteriostatic water (0.9% benzyl alcohol) slowly down the vial wall and swirl gently. Do not shake. A common choice is 2 mL per 10 mg vial (5 mg/mL; 0.01 mL = 1 unit = 0.05 mg) or 1 mL per 5 mg vial (5 mg/mL). Some guides use 5 mL per 10 mg (2 mg/mL).
- **Lyophilised storage:** -20 °C for long-term storage; 2-8 °C short term; protect from light and moisture (vendor guidance, for example BioEdge; TargetMol "keep away from moisture").
- **Reconstituted storage:** refrigerate at 2-8 °C, do not freeze, protect from light. Most sources say use within 14-28 days, and some say up to 28-30 days. These figures are vendor and community claims, not manufacturer or regulatory stability data. Discard if cloudy, discoloured or particulate.
- **Shelf life:** lyophilised material is typically claimed stable for 12-24 months frozen (vendor claims, unverified). Once reconstituted, about 4 weeks maximum.
- **Bacteriostatic water:** 2 mL per 10 mg vial is enough. A 30 mL bottle covers a full course, but discard multi-dose bacteriostatic water 28 days after first puncture (common practice; not separately verified).
- **Supplies:** U-100 insulin syringes and alcohol swabs.

### Purchase sizing and typical cost

Assuming the most commonly cited protocol (reference only):

- **8-week course:** 4 weeks of loading at 2.5 mg twice weekly (20 mg), then 4 weeks of maintenance at 2-2.5 mg weekly (8-10 mg). About 28-30 mg in total. Buy 3 x 10 mg vials.

### Verdict for general health

Weak case for a healthy adult taking it for "overall health".

- For injected TB-500 there are no human efficacy data at all.
- The human trials of the related full-length Tβ4 molecule (eye drops, topical gel) mostly missed their primary endpoints, though they showed reasonable short-term safety.
- TB-500's claimed benefits are injury and tissue-repair benefits, not general wellness.
- The main uncertainties are long-term safety (a theoretical pro-angiogenic or cancer-progression concern), product identity and purity in an unregulated "research use only" market, and FDA's own immunogenicity concern.

If the GP's goal is recovery from a specific soft-tissue injury, a time-limited trial (about 8 weeks, 3 x 10 mg vials) is the commonly cited approach. It largely overlaps with BPC-157: the only head-to-head animal study found no added benefit from using both.

If the goal is purely general health, TB-500 is the most dispensable item on the list.

Hard stops: cancer history, pregnancy, competitive athletes (WADA).

Regulatory status could change if FDA acts on the July 2026 PCAC vote. That would make pharmacy-compounded, quality-controlled supply possible through a prescriber.

## 4. CJC-1295 (no DAC)

**Evidence grade:** D for the no-DAC molecule itself. Pooled class evidence for GHRH analogues would be about C for "raises GH/IGF-1", and A only for tesamorelin in HIV lipodystrophy (a different molecule in a specific patient population).

### What it is

CJC-1295 without DAC is a synthetic 29-amino-acid analogue of growth-hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH). It is also known as Modified GRF (1-29).

Other names:

- CJC-1295 no DAC; CJC-1295 without DAC.
- Mod GRF 1-29; Modified GRF (1-29).
- Tetrasubstituted GRF(1-29); [D-Ala2, Gln8, Ala15, Leu27]-hGRF(1-29)-NH2.
- CJC-1295 free base / CJC-1295 acetate (the FDA nomination names).
- Often sold pre-blended as "CJC-1295 (no DAC) + Ipamorelin".

Background:

- It is sermorelin (GRF 1-29, the shortest fully active fragment of human GHRH) with four amino-acid substitutions at positions 2, 8, 15 and 27. These were made to resist enzymatic breakdown and extend half-life from roughly 10-20 min (native GRF 1-29) to about 30 min (source: Wikipedia "Modified GRF (1-29)", seen via search summary).
- It was developed and characterised by ConjuChem Biotechnologies as the peptide backbone of CJC-1295.
- ConjuChem's clinical work used the DAC (Drug Affinity Complex) version, which binds albumin and has a half-life of about 6-8 days.
- The "no DAC" version sold by research vendors is the short-acting backbone only. It is a different product from "CJC-1295 with DAC", and buyers should not confuse the two.
- It is not an FDA-approved drug.

### How it is thought to work

- It binds and activates the GHRH receptor on pituitary somatotroph cells (a Gs-coupled receptor that raises cAMP). This increases growth hormone (GH) synthesis and pulsatile GH release. GH then raises hepatic IGF-1.
- Because it works upstream of GH, somatostatin and IGF-1 negative feedback stay intact. Ionescu & Frohman 2006 found that even the long-acting DAC form preserved normal GH pulse frequency and size, with rises in trough GH and IGF-1.

**Structure-function of the four substitutions:**

- D-Ala2 resists dipeptidyl peptidase-IV (DPP-IV) cleavage at the Ala2-Asp3 bond, the main inactivation route of native GRF.
- Gln8 replaces Asn8 to reduce deamidation/isomerisation and improve stability in water.
- Ala15 is reported to improve receptor potency.
- Leu27 replaces Met27 to prevent oxidation.

**Synergy with GHRPs.** GHRH analogues act synergistically with ghrelin-receptor agonists (GHRPs such as GHRP-6 or ipamorelin). In humans, GHRH plus a GHRP given together releases far more GH than either alone (Bowers et al. 1990, JCEM 70:975). This is the rationale for the CJC/ipamorelin pairing.

**Pharmacokinetics.** Native GRF(1-29) plasma half-life is about 10-20 min. Mod GRF is reported at about 30 min (secondary, unverified against a primary PK paper). No human pharmacokinetic study of the no-DAC peptide alone has been published.

Claimed benefits, as marketed:

- Higher pulsatile GH and IGF-1 output ("more natural" GH release than exogenous GH).
- Fat loss, especially visceral or abdominal fat (a claim borrowed largely from tesamorelin trials).
- Lean mass and muscle gain, and better recovery from training.
- Deeper or better sleep (timed pre-bed to amplify the nocturnal GH pulse).
- Skin, hair and connective-tissue quality, "anti-aging".
- Faster injury healing (often stacked with BPC-157/TB-500 for this).
- Bone density (theoretical, via GH/IGF-1).

### What the evidence shows

**Human RCTs on this exact molecule (Mod GRF 1-29 without DAC): none published.** The analysts found no peer-reviewed human trial, dose-finding study or pharmacokinetic study of the no-DAC peptide alone. Community dosing sites acknowledge this. ConjuChem's human programme used the DAC conjugate.

**Closest human evidence (indirect, class-level):**

1. **CJC-1295 WITH DAC.** Two randomised, placebo-controlled, double-blind ascending-dose trials in 21-61-year-old healthy adults (Teichman 2006, JCEM 91:799).
   - One injection raised GH 2-10-fold for 6 days or more and IGF-1 1.5-3-fold for 9-11 days, with no serious adverse reactions.
   - These were endocrine endpoints only, with no body-composition, function or clinical outcomes.
   - Ionescu & Frohman 2006 (JCEM 91:4792) found preserved GH pulsatility and +45% IGF-1 at 1 week.
2. **Sermorelin (unmodified GRF 1-29).** Small human trials in older adults.
   - A 5-month single-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial (about 19 men and women aged 55-71) of [Nle27]GHRH(1-29) reported endocrine and metabolic effects (PubMed 9141536).
   - Corpas 1992 (JCEM 75:530) showed twice-daily GHRH(1-29) raised GH/IGF-1 in older men.
   - Body-composition results are mixed. One study reportedly found no change in fat or muscle (unverified).
3. **Tesamorelin** (an FDA-approved GHRH 1-44 analogue). Large phase-3 RCTs (Falutz NEJM 2007, n=412).
   - These show about 15-18% visceral-fat reduction in HIV-associated lipodystrophy, with IGF-1 rising within the physiological range and no significant glycaemic difference in the main trial.
   - This is the only grade-A evidence for any GHRH analogue, and it applies to a different molecule in a specific patient population.
4. **GHRH plus GHRP synergy** is well documented in human acute-dosing studies (Bowers 1990; a 1995 study showed it persists in older adults). These measured acute GH release only, not outcomes.

**Animal and in-vitro data.** The DAC form normalised growth in GHRH-knockout mice. Plasma-stability studies support the D-Ala2/Gln8/Leu27 substitutions.

**Anecdote.** Large community use (forums; a 2016 "netnography" of female CJC-1295 users, PMID 26771670, describes use but not efficacy).

**Gaps.**

- No long-term safety data.
- No outcome trials for fat loss, muscle, sleep, healing or longevity.
- No published human PK for the no-DAC form.
- No data in healthy middle-aged non-GH-deficient adults on hard endpoints.
- Raising GH/IGF-1 in someone without GH deficiency is not shown to improve health or longevity.

**Grade D** for the no-DAC molecule itself. Pooled class evidence for GHRH analogues would be about C for "raises GH/IGF-1" and A only for tesamorelin in HIV lipodystrophy. (Examine.com had no CJC-1295 page in the search results; whether one exists is unverified.)

Reduced visceral fat and improved lipids with no significant glycaemic difference. Strongest outcome evidence for any GHRH analogue, but for tesamorelin (a different molecule) in HIV lipodystrophy. ([link](<https://www.ovid.com/journals/nejm/pdf/10.1056/nejmoa072375~metabolic-effects-of-a-growth-hormone-releasing-factor-in>))
- **aidsmap (July 2006). Lipodystrophy study halted after patient death (ConjuChem CJC-1295 Phase II).** ConjuChem halted its 192-patient Phase II CJC-1295 (DAC) trial in HIV visceral obesity after a participant death. The investigator attributed it to likely pre-existing coronary disease, unrelated to drug, but development was terminated and results were never peer-reviewed. ([link](<https://www.aidsmap.com/news/jul-2006/lipodystrophy-study-halted-after-patient-death>))
- **FDA Briefing Document, Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee, 4 Dec 2024 (CJC-1295-related bulk drug substances).** FDA reviewed CJC-1295 free base, acetate, DAC free base, DAC acetate and DAC TFA and proposed that CJC-1295 NOT be included on the 503A Bulks List. Secondary sources report the committee voted against inclusion. ([link](<https://www.fda.gov/media/183583/download>))
- **Renehan AG, et al. Insulin-like growth factor (IGF)-I, IGF binding protein-3, and cancer risk: systematic review and meta-regression. Lancet 2004.** Higher circulating IGF-1 was modestly associated with higher risk of some cancers (prostate, premenopausal breast, colorectal in later pooled analyses). This is the basis for caution with any GH/IGF-1-raising agent in people with cancer history or high risk. ([link](<https://christie.openrepository.com/entities/publication/e85cccce-6513-4e55-b72a-f4aa2d2d366d>))

### Reference protocols

*Commonly cited reference ranges to discuss with the prescribing GP, not a recommendation. None of these doses has been validated in any published human trial of the no-DAC molecule.*

- **Route:** subcutaneous injection (abdomen or thigh) with an insulin syringe (U-100, 29-31G).
- **Common community dose:** 100 mcg per injection. This is often described as a "saturation" dose of about 1 mcg/kg. That idea comes from forum reasoning about GHRH dose-response, not a study. The reported range is 50-300 mcg per injection.
- **Frequency:** 1-3 times daily.
  - Minimalist protocol: once daily at bedtime.
  - Common protocol: twice daily (on waking and pre-bed).
  - Aggressive protocol: three times daily (adding post-workout).
- **Timing:** on an empty stomach, about 2 h after food and 20-30 min before eating. Carbohydrate, fat and insulin blunt the GH response. Pre-bed dosing aims to amplify the natural nocturnal GH pulse.
- **Pairing:** usually injected in the same syringe as ipamorelin, 100-200 mcg ipamorelin + 100 mcg Mod GRF. Pre-blended vials are also sold.
- **Schedule:** often 5 days on and 2 days off, or daily.
- **Cycle length:** 8-12 weeks, followed by 4 weeks or more off. This is anecdotal, intended to limit desensitisation and IGF-1 exposure. No data supports a specific cycle.
- **Monitoring a GP would reasonably consider:** baseline and on-cycle IGF-1 (keep within the age-adjusted reference range), fasting glucose/HbA1c, and symptoms of fluid retention or carpal tunnel. The tesamorelin label requires glucose and IGF-1 monitoring for the same drug class.
- **Lawful clinical alternatives for the GP to consider:**
  - Sermorelin, which can be compounded under 503A because its FDA-approved product (Geref) was withdrawn for non-safety reasons per a 2013 Federal Register notice.
  - Tesamorelin (Egrifta), which is FDA-approved, although its only labelled indication is HIV lipodystrophy.

### Side effects and contraindications

**Reported or expected side effects** (class effects of GHRH analogues; the no-DAC molecule has no formal adverse-event dataset):

- Injection-site redness, itch or pain.
- Transient flushing or warmth, head rush and headache shortly after injection (widely reported anecdotally).
- Water retention or peripheral oedema, arthralgia, myalgia, limb pain and paraesthesia/carpal-tunnel symptoms. These are the most common reactions (more than 5%) on the tesamorelin label, a GHRH analogue.
- Reduced insulin sensitivity or raised fasting glucose. Glucose intolerance or diabetes is a labelled warning for tesamorelin.
- Hypersensitivity reactions (tesamorelin label).
- Increased hunger or lethargy (anecdotal; more typical of the GHRP pairing).
- Unknown long-term effects of chronically elevated IGF-1. The tesamorelin label says these are unknown.
- Research-grade product risks: possible mislabelling, contamination or endotoxin, the wrong molecule (DAC vs no-DAC confusion), and wrong content per vial. There is no FDA quality oversight.
- In a 2006 ConjuChem Phase II trial of the DAC form, a death occurred. The investigator judged it unrelated (coronary plaque rupture), but the programme was terminated.

**Contraindications and cautions** (extrapolated from the tesamorelin and GH class labels):

- Active malignancy or recent cancer history. GH and IGF-1 are growth factors, and higher IGF-1 is epidemiologically associated with higher prostate, breast and colorectal cancer risk. A strong family history or a pending cancer screen is a reason for GP caution.
- Pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Disrupted hypothalamic-pituitary axis: pituitary tumour or surgery, head irradiation or trauma.
- Diabetes or prediabetes without monitoring.
- Diabetic retinopathy (a GH-class concern; unverified for this molecule).
- Acute critical illness (increased mortality was seen with GH in critically ill patients).
- Known hypersensitivity.
- Competitive athletes subject to drug testing (WADA S2-prohibited).

**Drug interactions** (class-level, not studied for this molecule; specifics unverified):

- Glucocorticoids may blunt the GH response.
- Diabetes medication may need adjustment because GH antagonises insulin.
- Somatostatin analogues oppose its effect.
- Oral oestrogen lowers the hepatic IGF-1 response.
- Stacking with exogenous GH, MK-677 or other secretagogues compounds the IGF-1 and glucose effects.

### Interactions with the other four

- **Ipamorelin.** Strongly complementary; this is the textbook pairing.
  - CJC no-DAC (GHRH receptor) and ipamorelin (ghrelin/GHS-R1a receptor) act on two different pituitary receptors.
  - Co-administration produces a synergistic GH pulse in humans, demonstrated for GHRH + GHRPs (Bowers 1990).
  - Ipamorelin is selective, with little cortisol or prolactin rise.
  - They are usually drawn into one syringe or bought as a pre-mixed blend, and a GP prescribing one almost always means both.
  - The redundancy concern is not here. It is with adding CJC-1295 WITH DAC, sermorelin, tesamorelin, MK-677 or GHRP-2/6 on top, which would duplicate the same axis.
- **BPC-157.** No known pharmacological interaction. Different mechanism (angiogenesis, nitric oxide signalling, tendon and gut repair in animal models).
  - Often stacked with CJC/ipamorelin in "recovery" protocols on the theory that GH/IGF-1 supports tissue repair. That theory is untested in humans.
  - Additive theoretical concern: both are pro-growth or pro-angiogenic, so a cancer history is a stronger contraindication for the stack than for either alone.
- **TB-500 (thymosin beta-4 fragment).** Same as BPC-157. Complementary in theory (actin regulation, cell migration), no known interaction, and an additive pro-angiogenic and pro-growth caution.
- **GHK-Cu.** No known interaction. Different mechanism (copper-peptide signalling for collagen and skin remodelling), unrelated to the GH axis. Some "anti-aging" stacks pair it with GH secretagogues for skin, with no combined data.
- **Overall.** Within the GP's list, CJC no-DAC + ipamorelin form one functional unit (the GH-axis pair). BPC-157 + TB-500 form a second unit (the healing pair). GHK-Cu stands alone. None of the five has been studied together in humans.

### Handling and storage

- **Vial sizes commonly sold:** 2 mg and 5 mg lyophilised vials are most common, with 10 mg also offered. Blends such as "CJC-1295 no DAC + Ipamorelin" (for example 5 mg/5 mg) are also sold. Check that the label says "no DAC" / "Mod GRF 1-29". The DAC form is a different product.
- **Reconstitution:** use bacteriostatic water (0.9% benzyl alcohol). Inject the water slowly down the inside wall of the vial and swirl gently. Do not shake. Worked examples (arithmetic):
  - 2 mg + 2.0 mL = 1 mg/mL, so 100 mcg = 0.10 mL = 10 units on a U-100 insulin syringe.
  - 5 mg + 2.5 mL = 2 mg/mL, so 100 mcg = 0.05 mL = 5 units.
  - 5 mg + 5.0 mL = 1 mg/mL, so 100 mcg = 10 units (easier to measure, but needs a vial with that headspace).
- **Co-injection with ipamorelin:** draw each separately into the same syringe, or reconstitute a blend vial.
- **Lyophilised storage:** about -20 °C, dry and dark, for long-term storage. Vendors say it is stable for weeks to months refrigerated and up to 24 months or more frozen (vendor claims, unverified).
- **Reconstituted storage:** refrigerate at 2-8 °C, protected from light, and do not freeze/thaw repeatedly. Reported in-use life is 14 days to 4-6 weeks. Sources conflict and none cite a stability study. A conservative approach is to use within about 2-4 weeks, or to size the vial so it is used within that window. The Gln8 substitution was designed to improve stability in water versus native GRF.
- **Bacteriostatic water:** typically sold in 10 mL or 30 mL vials. One 10 mL vial covers several peptide vials. Discard bacteriostatic water about 28 days after first puncture (standard multi-dose practice).

### Verdict for general health

Weak case for a healthy adult without diagnosed GH deficiency.

- CJC-1295 no DAC reliably raises GH/IGF-1 in humans by mechanism and class evidence.
- However, there are no published human trials of this exact molecule, and no outcome evidence for fat loss, muscle, sleep, healing or longevity in healthy people.
- Chronically raising IGF-1 carries theoretical cancer and glucose-metabolism downsides.
- It is not FDA-approved, it cannot lawfully be compounded under 503A (FDA proposed against Bulks-List inclusion in Dec 2024), and research-grade supply carries real quality risk. It is WADA-banned.

If the GP still wants a GH-axis intervention:

- CJC no-DAC only makes sense paired with ipamorelin. Treat the two as a single unit.
- It should be done with baseline and follow-up IGF-1 and glucose testing, no active or recent cancer, an 8-12-week cycle, and a vendor publishing per-lot third-party HPLC/MS certificates.

**A more defensible alternative to raise with the GP** is a lawfully compounded or prescribed GHRH analogue: sermorelin (503A-compoundable) or tesamorelin (FDA-approved, off-label outside HIV lipodystrophy).

Of the five peptides on the list, this pair has the most plausible measurable effect (raising IGF-1) but the weakest justification for "overall health". It is the most reasonable one to drop if the person wants to reduce cost and risk.

## 5. Ipamorelin

**Evidence grade:** D for the claimed general-health benefits. The narrow pharmacological claim that it acutely raises GH in humans would rate about C on its own.

### What it is

Ipamorelin is a synthetic 5-amino-acid peptide that acts as a growth hormone secretagogue (a ghrelin mimetic, also called a GHRP).

Other names:

- Ipamorelin acetate; ipamorelin (free base).
- NNC 26-0161 (the Novo Nordisk development code).
- Aib-His-D-2Nal-D-Phe-Lys-NH2 (the pentapeptide sequence; from background knowledge, not verified against an opened source).
- Often sold blended as "CJC-1295 (no DAC)/Ipamorelin" or "Mod GRF 1-29 + Ipamorelin".

Background:

- Novo Nordisk developed it in the 1990s from the GHRP-1/GHRP-6 family. Raun et al. 1998 called it "the first selective growth hormone secretagogue" (Eur J Endocrinol 139:552-561).
- It was later tested intravenously for postoperative ileus in a Phase 2 trial (NCT00672074). That trial missed its endpoints and development for the indication stopped.
- It is not approved as a medicine anywhere the analysts could confirm.
- In the US it is sold mainly as a "research use only" lyophilised powder. Before 2024 it was also sold through compounding clinics.
- The CAS number and molecular weight are left out as unverified.

### How it is thought to work

- It is an agonist at the growth hormone secretagogue receptor (GHS-R1a, the ghrelin receptor) on pituitary somatotrophs and in the hypothalamus. It triggers pulsatile GH release, which raises IGF-1 further downstream.
- Antagonist profiling showed it works through a GHRP-like receptor, not the GHRH receptor (Raun 1998).
- **Selectivity is its selling point.** In conscious swine it did not raise ACTH or cortisol above GHRH-stimulated levels, even at doses more than 200-fold above the GH-release ED50. GHRP-6 and GHRP-2 did. None of the secretagogues tested changed FSH, LH, prolactin or TSH in swine (Raun 1998 abstract, via a PubMed 9849822 mirror).
- **Human pharmacokinetics** (Gobburu 1999, IV 15-min infusions in healthy men, 5 dose levels, 8 subjects each): dose-proportional PK, terminal half-life about 2 h, GH peaking about 0.67 h after dosing, and EC50 about 214 nmol/L.
- **Feedback.** Because the GH release is pulsatile and stays under somatostatin/IGF-1 negative feedback, GH secretagogues are argued to be less likely than exogenous GH to cause supraphysiological GH (Sigalos & Pastuszak 2018 review).
- Ghrelin-receptor agonism also has prokinetic GI effects, which was the rationale for the ileus trial, and can increase appetite.

Claimed benefits, as marketed:

- Raises endogenous GH and IGF-1 in pulses without raising cortisol or prolactin (supported in animals plus human GH-release PK/PD; long-term IGF-1 outcomes in healthy adults not demonstrated).
- Improved body composition: fat loss and lean mass gain (no human trial found).
- Better sleep quality or deeper sleep (no human trial found; rated "preliminary" by secondary sites).
- Faster recovery from training and injury, and musculoskeletal healing (no human trial found; 2026 reviews call it unsubstantiated).
- Anti-aging, skin and hair, energy (anecdotal or marketing only).
- Bone growth or bone density (rat data only: longitudinal bone growth in rats, Johansen 1999).
- GI motility and postoperative ileus (Phase 2 RCT negative).

### What the evidence shows

**Human RCTs: one Phase 2 proof-of-concept RCT, in a different indication** (Beck et al. 2014, Int J Colorectal Dis 29:1527-1534; NCT00672074).

- Design: IV ipamorelin 0.03 mg/kg twice daily vs placebo after bowel resection. 117 enrolled, 114 in the mITT analysis.
- Result: median time to first tolerated meal was 25.3 h vs 32.6 h, p=0.15, which is not significant.
- The authors called it well tolerated, but it missed its efficacy endpoints and the indication was discontinued.
- A 2016 meta-analysis found ghrelin-receptor agonists had no apparent effect on postoperative ileus across five trials.
- No RCT of ipamorelin exists for any outcome the GP is prescribing it for: body composition, sleep, recovery, healing or longevity.

**Small human studies.**

- PK/PD dose-escalation in healthy male volunteers (Gobburu 1999, Pharm Res 16:1412-1416) shows it reliably releases GH acutely.
- There are no published long-term human data on IGF-1, body composition or safety with subcutaneous use.
- The FDA briefing for the Oct 2024 PCAC said FDA found no safety data for subcutaneous ipamorelin. FDA also found no nonclinical acute or repeat-dose toxicity, genotoxicity or carcinogenicity studies in the literature.

**The synergy rationale.** The GHRH + GHRP synergy used to justify pairing with CJC-1295 is shown in humans only for GHRP-6/GHRP with native GHRH (Bowers 1990, JCEM 70:975; 18 normal men). No published human study of the CJC-1295 no-DAC + ipamorelin combination was found.

**Animal and in-vitro data.**

- GH release with a selective hormone profile in rats and swine (Raun 1998).
- Longitudinal bone growth in rats (Johansen 1999, PubMed 10373343).
- Faster GI transit in a rodent postoperative ileus model (Venkova 2009/2016, PubMed 27186127).

**Anecdote.** The large majority of the "health" claims (sleep, fat loss, recovery, skin, anti-aging) come from clinic and vendor marketing and forums.

**2026 reviews.** The Sports Medicine 2026 narrative review (doi 10.1007/s40279-026-02437-0) and a 2026 AJSM-type scoping review (doi 10.1177/03635465261464420) both conclude that the musculoskeletal and performance claims for emerging peptides, ipamorelin included, are unsubstantiated by human trials, and that rigorous human safety data are scarce.

**Gaps.**

- No efficacy RCT for any wellness endpoint.
- No long-term safety data.
- No subcutaneous safety dataset.
- No data in women or older adults for wellness use.
- No data on research-grade products, whose identity, sterility and endotoxin are unregulated.

**Grade D** for the claimed general-health benefits. The narrow pharmacological claim that it acutely raises GH in humans would rate about C on its own.

### Reference protocols

*Commonly cited reference ranges to discuss with the prescribing GP, not a recommendation. No regulator-approved dose exists for any indication. The only human trial doses were IV and hospital-based (0.03 mg/kg twice daily in the ileus RCT), which says nothing about wellness use. Community and clinic figures come from vendor and clinic sites (for example perfectb.com), not trials.*

- **Route:** subcutaneous injection (abdomen or thigh) with an insulin syringe.
- **Dose:** the most commonly cited dose is 100-300 mcg per injection. A conservative starting point is 100 mcg. Sources disagree on whether 300 mcg is per peptide or the combined CJC + ipamorelin dose in a blend.
- **Frequency:** 1-3 times daily. A single bedtime dose is the most common wellness pattern. Two to three times daily is described as "aggressive". Many protocols use 5 days on and 2 days off.
- **Timing:** on an empty stomach (carbohydrate and fat blunt GH release), typically at bedtime 2 or more hours after the last meal, and/or on waking or after training.
- **When paired with CJC-1295 no DAC:** both are given in the same syringe or as a pre-mixed blend at the same time. A 1:1 mcg ratio of about 100 mcg each is typical.
- **Cycle:** 8-12 weeks on, then 4 or more weeks off, is the commonly cited pattern. Cycling is recommended in community sources but has no trial basis.
- **Outlier:** one Florida clinic blog cites a much higher escalating protocol starting at 0.6 mg/day. That is an outlier and unsupported.
- **Monitoring a prudent GP would consider:** baseline and on-treatment IGF-1 (keep within the age-normal range), fasting glucose/HbA1c, blood pressure and oedema, and age-appropriate cancer screening (for example PSA, mammography, colonoscopy) before starting.

### Side effects and contraindications

**Human data are thin.** Known or reported effects:

1. The IV ileus RCT reported it was well tolerated vs placebo.
2. FDA's Category 2 page and PCAC materials cite a published study with serious adverse events including death when ipamorelin was given IV for gastric motility. The source study and its causality could not be verified.
3. FAERS through Sept 2023 held 2 non-serious reports, both with compounded products (FDA PCAC briefing).
4. PCAC members raised class concerns about fluid retention, congestive heart failure and hyperglycaemia for GH secretagogues.
5. Class effect: reduced insulin sensitivity and higher blood glucose (Sigalos 2018).
6. FDA flags immunogenicity risk from subcutaneous injection due to aggregation and peptide impurities. It contains unnatural amino acids.
7. Anecdotal and vendor-reported: injection-site redness or itching, transient flushing or head rush, headache, increased hunger (ghrelin agonism), water retention, tingling or numbness in the hands (carpal-tunnel-like, a GH effect), and fatigue or lethargy.
8. Product-quality risk: "research use only" vials are not made under drug cGMP. Sterility and endotoxin testing are usually absent ("Sterility not tested unless listed" on one vendor page), and identity and dose can deviate from the label.

**Contraindications and cautions** (extrapolated from GH/IGF-1 since there are no ipamorelin-specific data):

- Active cancer or a history of malignancy. Growth hormone product labels carry a warning against use in active malignancy, and IGF-1 is epidemiologically linked to prostate, breast and colon cancer (PMC9861672, PMC9066587). GH replacement cohorts have been reassuring (SIR 0.92).
- Diabetes or prediabetes and insulin resistance.
- Heart failure or significant oedema.
- Untreated pituitary or hypothalamic tumour.
- Diabetic retinopathy.
- Pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Under 18 or still growing.
- Tested athletes (WADA).

**Drug interactions:**

- Additive GH/IGF-1 and glucose effects with any other GH secretagogue (CJC-1295, sermorelin, tesamorelin, MK-677) or with exogenous GH.
- May blunt the glycaemic effect of diabetes medications.
- Glucocorticoids blunt the GH response.
- No formal drug-drug interaction studies exist.

### Interactions with the other four

- **CJC-1295 (no DAC, that is Mod GRF 1-29).** The main synergy on the list.
  - CJC-1295 is a GHRH-receptor agonist and ipamorelin is a GHS-R1a/ghrelin-receptor agonist, so they act on two different pituitary pathways.
  - Human data show GHRH plus a GHRP gives synergistic, greater-than-additive GH release (Bowers 1990, with GHRP-6 and native GHRH, not these exact molecules). That is why the two are dosed together and sold as pre-mixed blends (for example 5 mg/5 mg).
  - They are pharmacologically complementary rather than redundant, but their side effects add up: IGF-1 rise, glucose, water retention, and the cancer-history contraindication.
  - Buying both separately allows independent titration. A blend fixes the ratio.
  - No human trial of the specific combination exists.
- **BPC-157.** No mechanistic overlap. BPC-157 is a gastric pentadecapeptide with angiogenic and tissue-repair claims (animal data). There are no interaction studies. Their "recovery" rationales are complementary only in theory. Both carry a theoretical tumour-promotion concern (angiogenesis plus IGF-1), so a cancer history argues against both.
- **TB-500 (thymosin beta-4 fragment).** No mechanistic overlap (actin-binding, cell migration). No interaction data. The same additive theoretical concern about growth signalling applies with any malignancy.
- **GHK-Cu.** No mechanistic overlap (copper-tripeptide, collagen and skin remodelling). No interaction data, and essentially independent.
- **Redundancy with similar peptides not on the list.** Sermorelin and tesamorelin overlap with CJC-1295 as GHRH analogues. GHRP-2, GHRP-6, hexarelin and ibutamoren (MK-677, oral) overlap with ipamorelin as ghrelin agonists. Using more than one from either class is redundant and raises side-effect risk.
  - Ipamorelin is preferred within its class for its reported cortisol- and prolactin-sparing profile (animal data), and it is milder on appetite than GHRP-6.
  - Oral MK-677 raised glucose and fluid retention in trials, per class concerns.

### Handling and storage

- **Vial sizes sold (US research vendors):** 2 mg, 5 mg and 10 mg lyophilised vials. 10 mg is the most widely listed size (IDUN, Void Research, Amino USA, Biolongevity Labs, Peptides Source, Oath, Licensed Peptides), and 5 mg is common. Larger sizes (for example 15 mg) and CJC-1295 no DAC/ipamorelin blends (for example 5 mg/5 mg) are also sold.
- **Reconstitution:** inject bacteriostatic water (0.9% benzyl alcohol) slowly down the vial wall and swirl. Do not shake. Worked examples on U-100 insulin syringes, where 100 units = 1 mL:
  - 10 mg + 2 mL gives 5 mg/mL, so 100 mcg = 2 units and 200 mcg = 4 units (small volumes, harder to measure).
  - 10 mg + 3 mL gives about 3.33 mg/mL, so 100 mcg is about 3 units.
  - 5 mg + 2.5 mL gives 2 mg/mL, so 100 mcg = 5 units and 200 mcg = 10 units (easiest to measure).
- **Lyophilised storage:** in the freezer at -20 °C for long-term storage. Vendors cite 24-36 months at -20 °C and up to about 90 days at 2-8 °C, but these are vendor figures, not stability studies.
- **Reconstituted storage:** refrigerate at 2-8 °C, protect from light, do not freeze. The most commonly cited use-by is 28 days after reconstitution (range given 21-28 days). Without bacteriostatic water some vendors claim about 14 days. No peer-reviewed or manufacturer stability study was found for ipamorelin, so all day counts are unverified.
- **Practical implication:** at 100-200 mcg/day a 10 mg vial lasts 50-100 days, which exceeds the 28-day window. Unless the user doses about 300 mcg/day or more, 5 mg vials (25-50 days at 100-200 mcg) waste less.
- **Bacteriostatic water:** each 10 mg vial needs about 2-3 mL. One 10 mL or 30 mL bottle covers a full course.

### Verdict for general health

Weak case on the evidence.

- Ipamorelin reliably produces an acute GH pulse in humans and appears hormonally "clean" (no cortisol or prolactin rise) in animals.
- Not one human trial shows it improves body composition, sleep, recovery, healing, skin or longevity. Its only RCT, for postoperative ileus, was negative.
- FDA's advisory committee voted 0-12 against allowing it in 503A compounding, citing no subcutaneous safety data, immunogenicity concerns and IV serious adverse events.
- It is not legally available through a US pharmacy as of October 2026, so it can only be bought as an unregulated "research chemical". It is WADA-prohibited.

If the user and GP proceed anyway, ipamorelin is the most reasonable partner for CJC-1295 no DAC, since the two act on complementary pathways and that is its main role on the list.

Practical guardrails from the research:

- Use the lowest cited dose (about 100 mcg SC at bedtime, 5 on/2 off).
- Run 8-12 weeks.
- Get baseline and follow-up IGF-1, fasting glucose/HbA1c and blood pressure.
- Complete age-appropriate cancer screening first.
- Do not use it with any personal cancer history, diabetes or prediabetes, heart failure, or during pregnancy.
- Buy only lots with a published third-party HPLC + MS certificate, ideally with endotoxin testing.

From an evidence standpoint, CJC-1295 no DAC and ipamorelin are the lowest-priority items on the list (both grade D). In a "stack" decision, the CJC/ipamorelin pair is the natural combination: buy both separately for dose flexibility, or as a 1:1 blend for convenience. The GHS/GHRH pair is optional for "general health", and an evidence-minded GP could reasonably drop it before any other item on the list.
